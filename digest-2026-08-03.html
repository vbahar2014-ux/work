⚡ Pattern of the Day
Day two of the enforcement era. The powers are vast — the question turns from "can they?" to "will they, at scale?"

The AI Office's new powers are, on paper, among the most far-reaching any government has claimed over frontier AI — documentation demands, independent model evaluations with source-code access, fines up to €15M / 3% turnover. But the analysis published as enforcement begins names the constraint: the Office is significantly under-resourced — a small GPAI unit, a thin evaluator pool, one report urging a scale-up to ≥160 staff by 2030. Near-term enforcement is good-faith-first and capacity-limited, with Code-signatory good faith the primary lever through the transition.

Around that line the market moved from agents in production to agents as a procurement decision: OpenAI now bills for agents, governance tooling is a six-figure line item, and buyers have codified an eight-area RFP + four agentic contract-clause families. The same discipline surfaced on cost — "FinOps for Agentic AI" is now a named Gartner category (98% of FinOps teams manage AI spend, up from 31%) — and on infrastructure, where JLL put a number on the demand curve: capacity nearly doubling to ~200 GW by 2030 on up to $3T, "a supercycle, not a bubble."

For Group Two actions this cycle: (1) adopt an agent-procurement standard — the eight-area RFP plus the four contract-clause families, with a termination-for-deprecation clause — before the next production agent contract; and (2) treat the AI Office's thin enforcement capacity as where-it-lands-first intelligence, not permission to slow readiness — confirm Group's foundation-model providers are Code signatories (the good-faith lever), verify chatbot / deepfake disclosure is live in production, and finish the AI-content marking inventory to 2 December.
